SolarWinds Orion SUNBURST supply-chain compromise

Attackers broke into SolarWinds, planted a tool in its build system that slipped the SUNBURST backdoor into signed Orion updates, and used those updates to reach thousands of customer networks in 2020. In a small set of chosen targets they went on to forge sign-in tokens and read email in cloud services. In April 2021 the US government formally named Russia's foreign intelligence service (SVR) as responsible.

  1. 1 Attackers get into SolarWinds' internal network

    Initial Access

    Russian Foreign Intelligence Service (SVR), aka APT29 → SolarWinds (maker of Orion)

    SolarWinds' own investigation traced the first suspicious activity on its internal systems to September 2019. An Orion release in October 2019 carried small test changes, which SolarWinds sees as the attackers checking whether they could slip code into its builds. The cited sources do not say how the attackers first got in. In April 2021 the US government formally named Russia's foreign intelligence service (SVR, also called APT29) as the actor, with high confidence.

    Sources: [4] [6]

  2. 2 SUNSPOT injects the backdoor during Orion builds

    Initial Access T1195.002

    Russian Foreign Intelligence Service (SVR), aka APT29 → SolarWinds Orion build system

    From February 2020 the attackers ran a tool, later named SUNSPOT, on the SolarWinds build system. It waited for Orion to be compiled, swapped one source file for a backdoored copy just before the compiler read it, and put the original back when the build ended. It checked file hashes first so a broken build would not give it away, which helped it stay hidden from the developers and build team.

    Sources: [4] [3] [9]

  3. 3 Signed, backdoored Orion updates reach customers

    Initial Access

    SolarWinds Orion build system → Customer Orion servers

    Because the code was added before the final build steps, the tampered Orion library (SolarWinds.Orion.Core.BusinessLayer.dll) was signed with SolarWinds' real code-signing certificate. CISA lists the affected releases as Orion 2019.4 HF5, 2020.2 RC1, 2020.2 RC2, 2020.2 and 2020.2 HF1, posted to SolarWinds' update site in spring 2020. Customers installed them as normal updates, and the backdoor, called SUNBURST, loaded inside the regular Orion service.

    Sources: [1] [3] [8] [7]

  4. 4 The backdoor waits and hides from defenders

    Stealth Reported

    Customer Orion servers → Customer Orion servers

    After install, SUNBURST stayed quiet for roughly 12 to 14 days. It held back if it found itself in a test or analysis setup or saw certain security and forensic tools, and it added random delays between check-ins. Its code used ordinary-sounding names and hidden text strings so it looked like normal Orion code. Mandiant also reported that commands were tucked inside normal-looking data in server replies; CISA said it had not confirmed that part itself.

    Sources: [7] [8] [1]

  5. 5 SUNBURST calls home and the attackers pick targets

    Command and Control T1071.004

    Customer Orion servers → Russian Foreign Intelligence Service (SVR), aka APT29

    Each infected server contacted its own subdomain of avsvmcloud[.]com, with traffic made to look like normal SolarWinds product traffic. The attackers used the DNS replies to choose which victims to pursue: chosen ones were sent new addresses for hands-on control, while many others got no follow-up. The control servers often used addresses in the victim's own country, and Mandiant reported hostnames copied from the victim's network.

    Sources: [1] [7]

  6. 6 Other ways in besides Orion

    Initial Access

    Russian Foreign Intelligence Service (SVR), aka APT29 → Customer and government networks

    CISA found the same actor inside some networks that had no Orion exposure. In some cases it got in by guessing or spraying passwords, or by using weakly protected admin credentials on remote access services. CISA warned that more entry methods were likely still unknown, so removing Orion alone was not enough.

    Sources: [1] [2]

  7. 7 Second-stage tools and quiet lateral movement

    Lateral Movement Reported

    Russian Foreign Intelligence Service (SVR), aka APT29 → Customer and government networks

    On selected victims the attackers loaded follow-on tools, including a memory-only dropper Mandiant named TEARDROP that in at least one case launched a customized Cobalt Strike Beacon. They moved between machines with credentials different from the ones used to get in, renamed admin tools, and briefly swapped legitimate utilities or scheduled tasks for their own before putting the originals back.

    Sources: [7] [8]

  8. 8 Stealing the SAML signing key to forge sign-ins

    Credential Access T1606.002

    Customer and government networks → Cloud identity and email (SAML, Microsoft 365)

    With high Active Directory privileges, the attackers took the certificate used to sign SAML sign-in tokens. They then made valid-looking tokens for services that trust that identity system, such as hosted email and file storage. CISA noted the actor relied heavily on stolen or forged tokens to move around.

    Sources: [1] [8]

  9. 9 Extra keys added in Microsoft cloud identity

    Persistence T1098.001

    Russian Foreign Intelligence Service (SVR), aka APT29 → Cloud identity and email (SAML, Microsoft 365)

    In Azure AD and Microsoft 365, the attackers added their own tokens and certificates to existing application service principals, giving them lasting, programmatic access through the Microsoft Graph API. They also added new federation trusts, which could keep their access alive.

    Sources: [1]

  10. 10 Reading and taking email through approved APIs

    Collection T1114.002

    Cloud identity and email (SAML, Microsoft 365) → Russian Foreign Intelligence Service (SVR), aka APT29

    The attackers went after the mailboxes of key staff, including IT and incident response people, and pulled data out through authorized cloud APIs.

    Sources: [1]

Impact

About 18,000 public and private sector Orion customers were affected, but US agencies said only a much smaller number saw follow-on intrusions, including fewer than ten US government agencies identified by January 2021. They believed the goal was intelligence gathering. According to the White House, the access could have let the SVR watch, and possibly disrupt, over 16,000 systems around the world. Mandiant reported victims in government, consulting, technology, telecom and extractive industries across several regions.

Sources: [5] [6] [7]
